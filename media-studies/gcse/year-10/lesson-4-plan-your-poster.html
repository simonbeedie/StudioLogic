Teach & Think — 40 min

Plan your own horror film poster

Read this section carefully, then start your own planning task below.

Over the last three lessons you've learned to read a poster like a Media Studies student. Today you flip it around: instead of analysing someone else's poster, you're going to plan your own — making the same kind of deliberate choices a real designer makes, and being ready to explain why you made them.

This is a planning task, not a drawing test. You do not need to be a brilliant artist. What matters is that every choice you make can be justified using the theory and terminology from Lessons 1–3.

Worked example: planning "Hollow Marsh"

Here's a planning template filled in for an imaginary film, modelled for you.

Hollow Marsh — a planning example

  1. Genre & iconography: Survival horror. Iconography: a rusted lantern, wading boots, thick fog, a half-submerged wooden sign.
  2. Character/representation decision: A lone teenage girl carrying the lantern — a Final Girl convention, representing resilience rather than helplessness.
  3. Semiotic codes: Signifier: the lantern's single weak flame. Connotation: fragile hope in overwhelming darkness.
  4. Typography & colour: Dripping, waterlogged-looking typography in murky green-grey, connoting decay and the wetland setting.
  5. Tagline: "Some places don't want to let you leave."
  6. Binary opposition: Light (the lantern, safety) vs. darkness (the marsh, danger).

Your turn: plan your own poster

Now it's your turn. Invent an original horror film — it doesn't need to be a real film, just a concept in your head. Answer the six planning questions below in your OneNote page.

Planning template

  1. Genre & iconography: What sub-genre is your film (e.g. slasher, survival, supernatural)? What props, characters or settings will signal this?
  2. Character/representation decision: Describe one character choice in your poster, and justify why you made it.
  3. Semiotic codes: What signifier(s) will appear on your poster, and what will they connote to your audience?
  4. Typography & colour: What font style and colour palette will you use, and why do they suit your genre?
  5. Tagline: Write a tagline for your film.
  6. Binary opposition: What binary opposition will structure your poster (e.g. hero vs. monster, safety vs. danger)?
This part is open to you

Once you've answered the six questions, choose one of the following:

Option A — Draw it: Sketch your poster concept on paper. Label it with your genre, semiotic and typography choices from your plan.

Option B — Write it: Write a short story or synopsis (150–300 words) for your film. Then explain underneath how you would translate your story into the poster choices from your plan.

Reflect & Review — 10 min

Pitch your poster

Get ready to share your idea with the class, or write a short pitch if you're presenting another day.

Prepare a 30-second pitch

  1. What's your film called, and what sub-genre is it?
  2. Describe your poster concept in one or two sentences.
  3. Name one theory or term from Lessons 1–3 that you used to make a specific choice, and explain that choice.

Before you finish: check your OneNote page has (1) your quiz score screenshot, (2) your completed six-question planning template, and (3) your drawn poster concept or written synopsis. This is the evidence your teacher will look at when they return.
