Check your findings after you have researched
  • ITV1: advertisers pay to show adverts around programmes. Bigger audiences mean ITV can charge more.
  • ITVX: a free tier paid for by adverts, plus a premium subscription tier with no adverts.
  • Scale: ITV’s total advertising revenue for 2021 was £2.1bn, and video-on-demand advertising grew by 41%.
  • BBC comparison: the BBC is publicly funded through the licence fee, so it does not rely on selling advertising.
  • Link to Trigger Point: both Trigger Point and The Sweeney are commercially funded, so large audiences matter to the broadcaster’s income.
  • Regulation: Ofcom regulates UK television and radio. It sets rules on harmful or offensive content and on protecting children, for example by keeping adult material for later in the evening. The Sweeney was shown after the 9pm watershed, and Trigger Point has a 15 certificate.
  • Public service: public service broadcasters such as the BBC have a duty to inform, educate and entertain. ITV is a commercial broadcaster but still has public service obligations as part of its licence.

Box 4 on your A3 sheet

Why does ITV make it this way?

Behind every programme are decisions about money, risk and realism. Your job is to find three of them and link at least one to a theory.

We couldn’t find a “making of” video for Trigger Point on ITVX. Check the series page in the app for extras. These articles are the next best way to see behind the scenes.

Two theories in plain language

Curran and Seaton: who owns and funds the media has power

The people and companies that own and pay for media can shape what gets made. If a broadcaster earns money from advertisers, it needs programmes that bring in large audiences.

Apply it: ITV needs big audiences to sell advertising, so it commissions gripping, mass-appeal thrillers.

Hesmondhalgh: cultural industries try to reduce risk

Making TV is expensive and nobody knows for certain whether audiences will like it. So companies reduce risk by using proven stars and creators, bringing series back, and selling programmes around the world.

Apply it: Vicky McClure and Jed Mercurio are proven names, and ITV commissioned a fourth series before the third had aired.

Exam practice

Section A, Question 2 is worth 10 marks and can test industries, audiences or contexts. Past papers have asked about convergence and distribution, how digital technology changes viewing, and historical context. Try these in the style of past papers.

Practice questions

Explain how Trigger Point shows the importance of media convergence and distribution. [10]

Explain how funding affects the way ITV makes crime dramas. Refer to Trigger Point. [10]

Explain how digital technologies change the way audiences watch television. Refer to The Sweeney and Trigger Point. [10]

Build each paragraph in four steps

  • PointMake a clear point that answers the question.
  • EvidenceGive a specific fact from Trigger Point or The Sweeney.
  • ExplainSay how the evidence proves your point.
  • LinkConnect to a theory, a context or the other programme.

Examiners reward specific links to the set product. General points about television that could apply to any programme earn little credit.

Model paragraph for question 1

Trigger Point shows the importance of convergence and distribution because ITV releases it on more than one platform at once. Series 2 premiered on ITV1 and ITVX together, so viewers could watch at a set time on a channel or stream it whenever they chose. This is convergence because traditional television and streaming work side by side.

It worked well for ITV: the series averaged 8.1 million viewers and was streamed 30 million times on ITVX. As a commercial broadcaster, ITV earns money from advertising on both ITV1 and the free tier of ITVX, so reaching people in more than one way helps it earn more. This contrasts with The Sweeney in 1975, which could only reach viewers on ITV at a fixed time.

Key terms

Commissioning

When a broadcaster decides to pay for a programme to be made.

Production company

The company that makes the programme: it hires the writers, directors, cast and crew.

Executive producer

A senior producer who oversees the whole project, often involved in finance and creative decisions.

Distributor

A company that sells programmes to broadcasters and platforms, often in other countries.

Broadcaster

The organisation that shows the programme to the public, such as ITV.

Public service broadcaster

A broadcaster with a duty to inform, educate and entertain, such as the BBC, which is funded by the licence fee.

Commercial broadcaster

A broadcaster that earns money mainly from advertising or subscriptions, such as ITV.

Licence fee

A yearly payment by UK households that mainly funds the BBC.

Linear TV

Programmes shown at a set time on a set channel.

Streaming (on demand)

Programmes watched when the viewer chooses, online, for example on ITVX.

Convergence

Different media forms joining together, such as TV channels and streaming services working side by side.

Appointment-to-view

A programme people make a point of watching at its scheduled time.

Regulation

Rules a media industry must follow. In the UK, Ofcom regulates broadcasting.

Cultural industries

Industries that make media and culture, such as TV, film and music (Hesmondhalgh).

Ofcom

The organisation that regulates UK television, radio and telecoms.

Watershed

The 9pm point after which broadcasters can show material that is unsuitable for children.

Age certificate

A rating such as 15 that shows the youngest audience a programme is considered suitable for.
