How is Lana represented?

Lana Washington is a competent female EXPO officer who is praised, yet marked out as “a woman in this job”. Your job is to explain how that representation is built and how audiences might read it.

The toolkit

Representation

The way producers select, construct and mediate a version of a person, group or idea. It is a version, not simply reality.

Binary opposition (Lévi-Strauss)

A pair of opposite ideas, characters or settings that a text’s meaning is built around, such as hero and villain.

Preferred reading (Hall)

The meaning producers intend most of the audience to take. Audiences can accept it, negotiate it or oppose it.

Check your findings after you have researched
  • Competent: Lana returns to EXPO duty and takes on a suspected device at a power station. She saves Thom when the device escalates to a pressure-plate explosive.
  • Marked out: Francis’s comment praises her but treats her gender as the headline, so she is “othered” even while being welcomed.
  • The kiss: a preferred reading sees a romantic payoff. An oppositional reading says it undercuts Lana’s professional story, because the climax ends on a relationship rather than her skill.
  • Conventions: the Thom, Helen and Lana triangle mixes professional competence with a romantic subplot, which is a common convention in female-led crime drama.
  • van Zoonen: a feminist reading asks whether women are shown as capable in their own right or always in relation to men and romance.
  • Producer intention: check the RTS article for how the actor and executive producer talk about the role.

Check your findings after you have researched
  • Hassan Rahim: steady, capable leadership. This is a positive representation of ethnicity in a senior role, because he is shown as competent rather than as a stereotype.
  • John Hudson: his hostility is framed through personal history, so it can be read as individual character. A negotiated reading asks whether it also reflects wider workplace attitudes to women.
  • Lana and Thom: past against present, with feelings unresolved. Their reunion is awkward because Thom arrives with his new partner.
  • The spec describes Trigger Point as a crime drama that includes gender and ethnic diversity, while using familiar crime conventions and extending them.
  • A strong judgement: “not very stereotypical” needs evidence from named characters, plus a counter-point, such as how few lines or how little screen time a character has.
  • bell hooks: intersectionality asks how different identities overlap, so one character’s experience may differ because of their gender, ethnicity and status together.
  • Gilroy: he argues that colonial ideas can still shape how ethnicity is represented, for example by placing minority groups as ‘other’. A positive representation avoids this by showing characters as capable, central and fully human.
  • Stereotypes: they help audiences understand characters quickly, but they can mislead. They can also change over time, so a character who breaks a stereotype may be making a point about society now.
  • Age: Lana returns to duty with experience but is tested by a changed team, and Thom has been promoted. Compare how experience, seniority and youth are shown, and whether any age group is missing or reduced to a stereotype.
  • Issues and events: a bomb threat in London is shown through specialist professionals, which constructs the police and EXPO teams as brave and technical. Note what the episode leaves out as well as what it shows.

Write in Box 3

  1. List every woman who appears in your extract and what she does. How much power does she have?
  2. Describe how the male detectives behave: how they talk, drink, smoke, argue and use violence.

Check your findings after you have researched
  • Women: there are few female characters. The only major female speaking role is Regan’s girlfriend, Jenny Peters, who has little agency and needs rescuing by Regan.
  • Roles: examiners expect women to be supporting characters, often victims or sexually objectified. All the senior officers and detectives are men.
  • Men: they lead all the action and solve problems with violence. Regan drinks, smokes and bends the rules, which reflects 1970s “macho” workplace culture.
  • Good and evil: there is a clear binary opposition between the police, who are the heroes despite using immoral means, and Frank Kemble’s criminal crew.
  • Context: the series reflects the Flying Squad before the Police and Criminal Evidence Act of 1984, when rule-breaking and violence were common and corruption was being investigated. The wider culture was patriarchal and unequal at work.
  • Hall across time: a 1975 audience might accept these representations as normal (preferred), but many viewers today take an oppositional reading because the women have so little power.

Model paragraph: how far does the representation of gender differ?

Trigger Point and The Sweeney represent gender very differently, although the contrast is not total. In The Sweeney the only major female speaking role is Regan’s girlfriend, who has little agency and needs rescuing, while the men lead every action. In Trigger Point the protagonist is a woman, Lana Washington, who is shown as a competent EXPO officer. This reflects a 2020s context of greater workplace equality, compared with the patriarchal and macho culture of the 1970s Flying Squad.

However, Trigger Point still marks Lana out as unusual. Commander Francis tells her it is great to see a woman in this job. The producers intend this as warm praise (a preferred reading), but a negotiated or oppositional reading could see it as patronising because it treats her gender as notable rather than normal. The closing kiss can likewise be read as a romantic payoff or as undercutting her professional story. So representation has changed significantly, but not completely.

Key terms

Representation

The way producers select, construct and mediate a version of a person, group or idea.

Stereotype

A simplified, widely held idea about a group of people.

Binary opposition

A pair of opposite ideas or characters that a text’s meaning is built around (Lévi-Strauss).

Preferred reading

The meaning the producers intend most of the audience to take (Hall).

Negotiated reading

The audience partly accepts the intended meaning and adapts it using their own experience.

Oppositional reading

The audience rejects the intended meaning.

Mediated

Passed through the choices of producers, such as what to show, how and in what order.

Agency

A character’s power to make choices and drive the story.

Othering

Treating a person or group as different from the norm.

Objectification

Showing a person mainly as an object of desire rather than as a full character.

Patriarchy

A society or workplace in which men hold most of the power.

Feminist perspective

A way of reading media that asks how women are shown and whether power is equal (van Zoonen).

Intersectionality

The idea that gender, race and class overlap and shape a person’s experience (bell hooks).

Context

The social, cultural, political or historical circumstances in which a product is made.

Postcolonial perspective

A way of reading media that asks how ideas from the colonial past still shape representations of ethnicity (Gilroy).

Under-representation

When a social group appears much less often than it does in real life.

Misrepresentation

When a group is shown inaccurately or unfairly, often through stereotypes.

Function of stereotypes

They let audiences understand a character quickly, but can reduce people to a few simple traits.

Quick quiz

Say the answer first, then open it to check.

1. What does “representation” mean?

The way producers select, construct and mediate a version of a person, group or idea, rather than simply presenting reality.

2. Define preferred, negotiated and oppositional reading.

Preferred: accepts the intended meaning. Negotiated: partly accepts and adapts it. Oppositional: rejects it.

3. Give a preferred and an oppositional reading of Francis’s line.

Preferred: warm, supportive encouragement. Oppositional: patronising, revealing casual sexism.

4. Name one binary opposition between Lana and Hudson.

Competence against resentment, or calm expertise against hostility.

5. Who is Jenny Peters and how is she represented?

Regan’s girlfriend in The Sweeney, the only major female speaking role, shown with little agency and needing rescue.

6. Give two ways the representation of gender differs between the two series.

A woman leads Trigger Point while The Sweeney’s senior roles are male. Lana is a competent professional, while women in The Sweeney are supporting characters.

7. Give one way the two series are similar in representing gender.

Both feature male authority figures, and Trigger Point still marks Lana out by gender and ends on a romantic moment.

8. What does Gilroy say about representations of ethnicity?

Colonial ideas can still shape representations today, for example by placing minority groups as ‘other’.

9. Why do producers use stereotypes, and what is the risk?

They help audiences understand characters quickly, but they can mislead and reduce people to simple traits.
