Overview

Every media product makes choices about how to show people, places, groups and events. Representation is the study of those choices — how the media constructs a version of reality for an audience, rather than simply recording it.

A photograph, a magazine cover or a television drama never shows us "the truth" directly. It shows us a version of events, built through choices: who is included, who is left out, what camera angle is used, what words are chosen, what comes first.

Stuart Hall's theory of representation is one of the most useful tools you have for analysing this construction — and it applies just as well to a bomb-disposal drama as it does to a magazine cover.

Key Ideas

Encoding and Decoding

Hall argued that meaning in media is built in two stages:

  • Encoding — the producer builds meaning into a text using media language choices (images, words, layout, camera work, casting). This is where the producer's intended, or "preferred", meaning comes from.
  • Decoding — the audience interprets that text, drawing on their own experiences, values and cultural background. Different audience members can decode the same text differently.

Three Ways an Audience Can Decode a Text

  • Preferred reading — the audience accepts the meaning the producer intended.
  • Negotiated reading — the audience broadly accepts the intended meaning but adapts parts of it to fit their own experience or views.
  • Oppositional reading — the audience rejects the intended meaning and interprets the text in a different, often opposing, way.

Stereotyping and "Othering"

Hall was especially interested in how the media represents social groups — particularly groups who are less powerful in society. He argued that repeated, simplified representations (stereotypes) can shape how audiences see whole groups of people, not just the individuals shown.

He used the term "Othering" to describe how media can present a group as different from, or opposite to, an assumed "normal" audience — often reinforcing existing power structures rather than challenging them.

Why this matters for exam analysis: don't just describe who or what is shown. Explain the choice being made, and what preferred meaning it constructs for the audience. Always ask: whose version of events is this? Who benefits from this representation? Who might read it differently?

Applying the Theory: Trigger Point (Series 2, Episode 1)

Worked example — read this through before the quiz.

The text

Trigger Point (ITV) follows Lana Washington, an EXPO (bomb disposal officer) in the Metropolitan Police and an Afghan War veteran. In the Series 2 opener, Lana has just returned from a secondment training bomb disposal teams abroad and has not yet returned to active duty. While giving a talk to security officers, an explosion hits a power plant in the city, pulling her straight back into frontline work under a new, more dismissive boss.

Applying Hall

Casting a woman as the lead expert in a high-risk, traditionally male-coded role is an encoding choice that constructs a preferred meaning of female competence and authority in a dangerous, technical field — challenging a long-standing stereotype of action and crisis roles as male territory.

The introduction of a new boss who is dismissive toward Lana because of her gender is itself a representational choice: it names and puts on screen the institutional sexism she has to work against, inviting the audience to side with Lana against that attitude rather than accepting it as normal.

A preferred reading accepts Lana as a credible, capable authority figure and reads the new boss's attitude as outdated and unfair. A negotiated reading might admire Lana's competence while still finding the "tough female lead" characterisation a little familiar from other crime dramas. An oppositional reading might reject the show's framing altogether — for example, seeing the sexist-boss subplot as a token gesture rather than a genuine challenge to how women are represented in action genres.
