Semiotics

Your standing reference for Barthes' semiotic toolkit. Come back to this page every time a task asks you to analyse denotation, connotation or myth — the terms below don't change from text to text, only the examples do.

01

Sign

Anything that carries meaning to someone who interprets it — an image, a word, a sound, an object, even a colour or a gesture. A sign is made up of two parts working together: the signifier and the signified.

In the Tide advert

The packet, the woman, the kitchen setting and the colour white are all individual signs, each doing separate interpretive work.

02

Signifier

The physical form of the sign — what you can actually see, hear or touch. It is the vehicle for meaning, not the meaning itself.

In the Tide advert

The bright white box design is the signifier; on its own it is just shapes and colour on paper.

03

Signified

The mental concept that the signifier triggers in the reader's mind. It is the idea, not the object — and it can shift depending on culture and context.

In the Tide advert

The whiteness of the packaging signifies the concept of "cleanliness" in the reader's mind.

04

Denotation

The literal, surface-level meaning of a sign — a plain, descriptive account of what is physically shown, before any cultural interpretation is added.

In the Tide advert

A woman is smiling and holding a box of washing powder in a kitchen.

05

Connotation

The associated, culturally learned meaning a sign carries beyond its literal description. Connotations are not fixed — they depend on shared cultural knowledge, so two readers can take different connotations from the same sign.

In the Tide advert

The smiling woman connotes domestic pride and competence, not just "a person who is happy."

06

Myth

Barthes' term for a connotation that has become so widely repeated and naturalised that it no longer feels like an idea at all — it feels like plain, neutral "common sense." Myth is how ideology hides inside media texts.

In the Tide advert

Cleanliness comes to stand for moral virtue, and domestic mastery comes to stand for feminine success — presented as simply "how things are," not as a constructed value.

07

Anchorage

The way written text (a caption, slogan or headline) fixes or narrows the possible connotations of an image, steering the reader towards one specific, intended meaning rather than leaving it open.

In the Tide advert

The slogan anchors the image of the woman and packet towards a specific reading: that Tide itself is the cause of her domestic success.

Going further · the theory behind the toolkit

Saussure & the Arbitrary Sign

Ferdinand de Saussure, often called the father of semiotics, is where signifier and signified come from. His key insight: the link between a signifier and its signified is arbitrary — there is no natural reason a particular form has to carry a particular meaning. The same signified concept can be attached to completely different signifiers depending on language or culture, and each one only works because a community has agreed to read it that way.

Signifier (English word)

Apple

Signified: the concept of an apple

Signifier (French word)

Pomme

Signified: the same concept of an apple

Signifier (brand logo)

 

Signified: a technology brand, not a fruit — same basic shape, entirely different signified once context changes

Why this matters for analysis

Because the sign is arbitrary, meaning is never fixed or natural — it's constructed and learned. That's exactly the gap Barthes' idea of myth exploits: a constructed, arbitrary meaning gets repeated so often it starts to feel natural instead.

Barthes' Five Codes

Beyond myth, Barthes argued that producers build meaning into a text through five codes working together. Most useful for narrative analysis (Section B), but worth recognising here too.

Hermeneutic code (enigma)

Anything left unexplained, creating a question the audience wants answered. Barthes names three ways a producer withholds information: snares (avoiding the truth), equivocations (a mix of truth and avoidance) and partial/suspended answers (deliberate half-reveals).

e.g. Broadchurch reveals its central mystery slowly across a series.

Proairetic code (action)

Any action that implies a further action is coming, building suspense.

e.g. A gun being raised in a scene implies it may be fired.

Cultural code (referential)

References that rely on the audience's shared world knowledge — historical, medical, religious, political.

e.g. The Simpsons referencing religion or politics.

Semantic code

An element that suggests connotation beyond its literal meaning. Each connotative element is called a seme.

e.g. A character running suggests fear, even though "running" only denotes movement.

Symbolic code

Similar to the semantic code, but working through binary opposites — good vs evil, order vs chaos.

e.g. A hero only reads as heroic because a villain defines the opposite.

Connects to: Lévi-Strauss — binary oppositions

Claude Lévi-Strauss argued audiences make meaning by recognising a difference between two opposing ideas — we understand "hero" because we understand "villain" as its opposite. This is the theory underneath Barthes' symbolic code, and it's the theorist to name if a question asks specifically about oppositions rather than myth.

Peirce's Three Categories of Sign

Charles Peirce offers a different way of classifying signs, based on how closely a signifier resembles or connects to what it represents.

Icon

Physically resembles what it signifies, so it needs no learning to interpret.

e.g. A portrait resembles the person it depicts.

Index

No visual resemblance, but a real, inferred connection to what it signifies.

e.g. A fingerprint is only an imprint, but it's indicative of a specific person's identity.

Symbol

No physical connection at all — meaning is a learned, agreed convention.

e.g. A wedding ring communicates marriage only because that meaning has been culturally agreed.

Naturalisation & Critique

Barthes argues that ideology is built through naturalisation: when a connotation repeats often enough across dominant media texts, audiences stop experiencing it as a constructed idea and start experiencing it as simple "common sense." That naturalised belief — widely held, but not actually true or neutral — is what Barthes calls a myth.

A useful evaluation point

Barthes' model has been criticised for focusing too heavily on one intended meaning and underplaying the fact that audiences can misread or resist a text (worth linking to Hall's reception theory in Section B). It's also been argued that digital media gives audiences more visible space to contest a text's myths than they had when Barthes was writing — useful to raise this as evaluation in an exam answer, not just description.

See It In Action

Four more adverts to practise on. For each, two signs have been broken into denotation and connotation, then pulled together into the ad's underlying myth — the same three moves you're using on the Tide advert.

Colgate — Enamel Health

“Smile with strength”

Gritted teeth and boxing wrap

Denotation: a woman bites down on a hand-wrap, teeth bared, muscles tensed

Connotation: physical strength, resilience, athletic toughness

Product packaging

Denotation: two tubes of toothpaste with clinical blue design and the word “Mineral Repair”

Connotation: scientific credibility, clinical trust

Myth: physical toughness and independence are naturalised as an aspirational identity — strength of body comes to stand for strength of character, and the toothpaste is positioned as the source of both.

Heinz — Tomato Ketchup

“No one grows ketchup like Heinz”

Tomato-slice bottle

Denotation: stacked tomato slices form the silhouette of a ketchup bottle, wrapped in the Heinz label

Connotation: freshness, natural origin, an almost home-made purity

Saturated red background

Denotation: deep red fills the entire frame

Connotation: ripeness, appetite, intensity of flavour

Myth: a mass-manufactured, factory-produced condiment is naturalised as identical to, and as wholesome as, the raw tomato it's made from — the industrial process behind the product disappears from view.

Persil — Power Gel

“No powder residues”

Suds across the shirt

Denotation: white foam appears to spread across his collar and shoulders as he strains upward

Connotation: the product's cleaning power made visible, almost superhuman

High-rise, urban backdrop

Denotation: skyscrapers behind a man working at height on a building's exterior

Connotation: physical labour, risk, everyday masculine grit

Myth: the detergent is naturalised as heroic and essential — powerful enough to match the toughest, most demanding version of modern working life.

Persil — Bio

“Outstanding stain removal in cold washes”

Strained expression

Denotation: gritted teeth, closed eyes and visible effort as she lifts a barbell

Connotation: intensity, athletic commitment, an active lifestyle

Motion lines

Denotation: curved white lines sweep around her head and shoulder

Connotation: energy, dynamism, power

Myth: a “powerful” modern life — athletic, busy, physically demanding — is naturalised as requiring an equally powerful detergent, merging domestic labour with an aspirational, athletic femininity.

Roland Barthes

French semiotician whose work on myth and connotation underpins this framework. "Barthesian" analysis means moving through the seven terms above in order: sign, denotation, connotation, myth.

Ferdinand de Saussure

Regarded as the father of semiotics. Established that a sign is made of a signifier and signified, and that the link between them is arbitrary rather than natural.

Charles Peirce

Classified signs by how closely they resemble what they represent: icon, index and symbol — a useful alternative lens to Saussure's model.

Claude Lévi-Strauss

Argued audiences understand meaning through binary oppositions — recognising a hero depends on recognising a villain as its opposite.
