Showing data as pictures

A page full of numbers is hard to read. A picture lets you see the answer at a glance.

Data visualisation is the graphical representation of data. In plain words, it means showing data as a picture, such as a chart, graph or map.
Dashboard: a short, simple visual summary of data, put on one screen using graphs, charts or maps. Think of the dashboard in a car: the most important facts, all in one view.

A dashboard puts several small visuals together so you can understand a lot very quickly. It is different from a single chart.

Software: you do not need special programs to start. A spreadsheet such as Excel or Google Sheets can turn data into charts in a few clicks. When a question asks which software, tool or package makes a basic visualisation, think spreadsheet. When it asks which chart or graph to use, you need to match the chart to the job (next section).

“compare two years”, “compare two groups for each category”Side-by-side (grouped) bar chart: two or more bars next to each other for each category
“trend”, “changes over time”, “daily”, “monthly”, “over a year”Line graph
“parts of a whole”, “share”, “percentage of the total”Pie chart
“relationship between two things”, “patterns between two variables”Scatter graph
Why not use a bar graph for everything? A bar graph shows separate groups, such as five teams. A line graph joins points in time order, so the eye follows the rise and fall. That is why a line graph is best for trends.

Cleaning data

Real data is messy. Before you chart or analyse it, you must tidy it. Skip this and your charts could lie.

Data cleaning is the process of finding and removing (or fixing) errors and gaps in a data set. It is how raw data is refined so that it is ready for analysis. Cleaning deals with:
  • missing values: an empty cell where a number should be;
  • symbols instead of numbers, such as # or ?;
  • inconsistencies: the same thing written in different ways, such as “two” and “2”.
What to do first? You cannot fix a problem you have not found. So the first step with messy data is to spot the problems (look through the data and find the incomplete or odd entries). Then you clean them: fix them, fill them in, or remove them.

Types of data, and putting data in order

Quantitative data is numerical: you can count or measure it, such as height or the number of goals.
Qualitative data is not numerical: it describes qualities, such as eye colour or favourite food. It is also called categorical data.

Sorting means arranging data into a meaningful order: smallest to largest, largest to smallest, or A to Z. It makes the biggest, smallest and middle values easy to find, and it helps you spot patterns.

Outliers: the extreme values

Outlier: a data value that is much higher or much lower than most of the others, so it looks very different from the rest. Different questions describe them in different ways: “extreme values”, “values far from the others”, “significantly different data points”. They all mean outliers.

Outliers can be mistakes that need cleaning, or real and interesting results. Either way, you must notice them before you trust your answers.

How strong is the link?

Two variables might be linked. But how closely? That is the strength of the correlation.

Correlation describes the relationship between two variables. The strength of a correlation tells you how closely the dots follow a pattern. Strong means the dots are tightly packed near a line. Weak means they are loosely scattered. Direction (positive or negative) is a separate idea.

Predicting what comes next

Past data can help us make good guesses about the future.

Forecasting is using past data to predict future trends. To forecast, data scientists build a model: a simple set of rules or a mathematical pattern based on what happened before. This is sometimes called predictive modelling.
