The big picture

Every time you watch a video, play a game or tap a map, data is made. A data scientist uses that data to answer questions.

Data science is the study of data so that we can get knowledge from it. Knowledge means useful facts that help us make good choices.

Four jobs you need to know

Data analysis

Looking carefully at data to answer a question. Many people do basic data analysis in a spreadsheet. A spreadsheet is a grid of rows and columns that can sort numbers and work things out. Excel and Google Sheets are two well-known examples.

Data mining

Digging through lots of data to find patterns and information that you would not spot by just looking. Think of mining for gold: you sift through tonnes of rock to find a few shiny pieces.

Machine learning

A method where a computer uses algorithms (sets of step-by-step rules) to learn patterns from data. The more data it sees, the better it gets. This is how a video app learns which clips you like.

Data visualisation

Showing data as a picture, such as a chart or graph. A picture lets our brains spot patterns much faster than a long list of numbers.

Watch out: “data mining” and “machine learning” are close cousins. Data mining is about finding the patterns. Machine learning is about a computer using algorithms to learn from them. Read each quiz question closely to see which one it describes.

The steps of a data science project

Data scientists follow steps in order. The order matters!

  1. 1
    Ask a question. Decide what problem you want to solve. This is always the starting point, because it tells you what data you need. Example: “Do students who sleep more feel less tired in lessons?”
  2. 2
    Collect data. Find or gather the numbers and facts. A survey is one way.
  3. 3
    Check and clean the data. Make sure it is correct and fix the mistakes (see the next section).
  4. 4
    Analyse it. Look for patterns and relationships.
  5. 5
    Visualise it. Draw a chart or graph.
  6. 6
    Share what you found. Explain your answer to the question you started with.

Getting data ready

Data is often messy. People make typing mistakes, leave boxes empty or write silly answers. If we use bad data, we get bad answers.

Data validation

Checking that the data is accurate and good enough to use. We check it against rules, such as “age must be a number between 11 and 14”.

Data cleaning

Finding and removing (or fixing) errors in the data. Cleaning makes sure the data is accurate and useful for analysis.

How to tell them apart: validation is like a check-up, because you test whether the data is OK. Cleaning is like the treatment, because you fix what is wrong. They work together.

Charts and graphs

A chart or graph is the most common way to show data. The big advantage is that you can see patterns and compare things quickly, without reading every number.

Different charts do different jobs. Choose a job below to see which chart fits.

Software for charts: spreadsheet programs such as Excel and Google Sheets can draw charts from your data in a few clicks. Specialist visualisation tools, such as Tableau and Power BI, do the same job for much bigger data.

Relationships between data

Data scientists often ask: “When one thing changes, what happens to the other?”

Correlation is the word for the relationship between two variables. A variable is anything that can change or be measured, such as height, age or the score in a game. When we talk about the general relationship between two variables, we are talking about their correlation.

The best graph for this is a scatter graph (also called a scatter plot or scatter diagram). It shows the relationship between two quantitative variables. Quantitative just means they are numbers you can measure or count. Each dot is one person or one thing.

Try it: three kinds of correlation

Positive correlation

Both variables go up together. The dots slope upwards from left to right.

Negative correlation

One variable goes up while the other goes down. The dots slope downwards from left to right.

No correlation

There is no relationship between the variables. The dots are scattered with no pattern.

What is a trend?

A trend is a pattern you can see in a data set, such as a gradual rise or fall. If the number of steps you walk each week keeps going up, there is an upward trend. A line graph is great for showing trends over time.

Careful! Correlation does not always mean that one thing causes the other. The number of umbrellas sold and the number of puddles in town go up together, but umbrellas do not make puddles. The rain causes both.
Quick think: what kind of correlation is it?

Look at these three pairs. Say positive, negative or none, and explain why.

A) Hours spent practising piano and number of mistakes in a performance.
B) Length of a bus journey and the price of the ticket.
C) Your birthday month and your score in a spelling test.

Answers: A is negative (more practice, fewer mistakes). B is positive (longer journey, higher price). C is none (they have nothing to do with each other).

The middle value

When we have a lot of numbers, we need one number that tells us what is typical.

Median is the middle value in a list of data values that has been sorted (put in order from smallest to biggest).

Outliers

Sometimes one data point is very different from all the others.

Outlier: a data point that does not fit the pattern of the rest of the data. It stands out, usually because it is much higher or much lower than the other values.

Why do we look for outliers?

It might be a mistake

Maybe someone typed 120 instead of 12. Finding the outlier lets us check and fix it.

It can change the results

One very big or very small value can pull the answers away from the truth. This could lead us to a wrong conclusion.

It might be interesting

Sometimes the outlier is real and tells us something special, such as a person who is truly amazing at something. We should find out why.

Remember: an outlier is not always wrong. Do not just delete it. First ask, “Is this a mistake, or is it real?”

Key words

Say each word out loud and explain it to a partner without looking.

Data science
The study of data to get knowledge from it.
Data analysis
Examining data carefully to answer a question. Spreadsheets are often used for the basics.
Data mining
Searching large amounts of data to find patterns and information.
Machine learning
Using algorithms so a computer can learn patterns from data.
Data validation
Checking that data is accurate and good quality.
Data cleaning
Finding and removing or fixing errors in data.
Visualisation
Showing data as a chart or graph so patterns are easy to see.
Bar graph
Compares amounts in different groups.
Line graph
Shows how something changes over time (a trend).
Pie chart
Shows parts of a whole.
Scatter graph
Shows the relationship between two number variables.
Correlation
The relationship between two variables.
Positive correlation
Both variables increase together.
Negative correlation
One increases while the other decreases.
No correlation
There is no relationship between the variables.
Trend
A pattern in data, such as a gradual increase or decrease.
Median
The middle value in a sorted list.
Outlier
A data point that does not fit the pattern and stands out from the rest.
